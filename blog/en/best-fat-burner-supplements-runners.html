Best Fat Burner Supplements for Runners in 2026

An honest guide and evidence-based ingredients for runners, triathletes and hybrid athletes who want to lose fat without hurting performance.

Let's be clear from the first line: no "fat burner" makes you lose meaningful fat on its own. The US NIH Office of Dietary Supplements concludes that the evidence for most of these products is weak. What really reduces body fat is energy balance, enough protein, strength training, activity volume, sleep and adherence to the plan. None of that comes in a capsule.

So why this guide? Because if you are going to consider a thermogenic as a complement to a solid base, you deserve to know which ingredients have real evidence, which are marketing, how much caffeine they carry and which risks matter for a runner. We reviewed 8 products available in Spain — with a special focus on label transparency — including the AMIX Burn & Dry, and we ranked them on verifiable criteria, not commission.

Quick answer

If you want the real effect without paying for smoke: the most honest option is usually the simplest — caffeine in an adjustable dose. Among the ingredients in this comparison, it is the one with the most consistent evidence for improving specific aspects of performance; its effect on fat loss is small.

If you want a transparent thermogenic formula: HSN Evoripped and AMIX Burn & Dry declare all their doses, which is rare in this category. Watch out: the AMIX includes a strong diuretic block, which sheds water, not fat.

If you are caffeine-sensitive or train at night: a stimulant-free option (L-carnitine) won't interfere with sleep, though its evidence for fat loss is weak.

Most important: supplements have a secondary effect versus diet, strength, rest and consistency. A fat burner won't fix a badly planned approach, and losing weight aggressively can ruin your training. Start with the base.

What a fat burner really is

Under the "fat burner" or "thermogenic" label sit very different supplements: some provide caffeine and other stimulants; others add plant extracts, diuretics or supposed "metabolism boosters". The name promises a lot, but the real mechanism is modest: at most, some ingredients slightly raise energy expenditure or fat oxidation, mildly reduce appetite, or improve energy and focus in a session. None "melts" fat.

What they can and can't do

What some can do (modestly, and not for everyone): provide energy and focus before training (caffeine), slightly lower perceived exertion, raise thermogenesis a little and, in some contexts, help with appetite.

What they cannot do: cause meaningful fat loss on their own, burn "spot fat" (fat loss is always global), replace a calorie deficit, or guarantee results. And a key point: many products that make the scale drop fast do it through water loss (diuretics), not fat; that weight returns when you rehydrate.

Ingredients with the best evidence

  • Caffeine: the best-supported ingredient, but on performance (~2-4% endurance gains at 3-6 mg/kg), not on fat. Its thermogenic and appetite effect is small and fades with habitual use.
  • Green tea extract (EGCG): combined with caffeine, small effects (around −0.4 kg vs a caffeine control); without caffeine, no significant effect. EFSA warns that ≥800 mg/day of EGCG from supplements may be associated with signs of liver injury.
  • Capsaicin/capsinoids: raise energy expenditure by around 50-70 kcal/day, mainly in overweight people; the effect on weight is marginal.

Ingredients with weak evidence or overblown marketing

  • Synephrine (bitter orange): small effect; the caution is the combination with caffeine (raised blood pressure and heart rate). Bitter-orange formulas are also a frequent vehicle for contamination in this category.
  • L-carnitine: weak, inconsistent evidence for fat loss via oral dosing.
  • Chromium (picolinate): a Cochrane review estimates −1.1 kg, of "debatable clinical relevance" and low-quality evidence.
  • CLA: a small effect that disappears in high-quality studies.
  • Garcinia cambogia (HCA): weak-to-null evidence and, importantly, signs of liver injury in published cases. We do not recommend it.
  • Choline: "rapid loss" data come from weight-class sports and reflect body mass/water, not fat.
  • Black pepper (piperine/BioPerine®): not a burner; it's an absorption enhancer. It can raise the bioavailability of other ingredients... and of medications too (interaction risk).
  • Plant diuretics (horsetail, dandelion, nettle): they shed water, not fat; for a runner, they compromise hydration and electrolytes.
  • Proprietary blends: they hide the dose of each ingredient. You can't tell if something is under-dosed or if you are stacking too many stimulants, and safety or interactions can't be assessed.

Fat burners with caffeine vs stimulant-free options

Most "burners" are, at heart, caffeine with companions. A formula with caffeine (HSN Evoripped, AMIX Burn & Dry, Weider) can give you energy before training, but it adds to your total daily caffeine and may affect sleep. A stimulant-free option (L-carnitine, CLA) won't interfere with rest, but its evidence for fat loss is weak. There is no universal choice: it depends on your sensitivity, your training schedule and how much caffeine you already have.

Risks of combining several products

Don't stack stimulants without adding up. It's easy to combine coffee + pre-workout + caffeinated gels + a fat burner and unknowingly exceed reference amounts. EFSA puts safe intake around 400 mg per day and 200 mg in a single dose for healthy adults, and a 100 mg dose near bedtime can already disrupt sleep. Add that many burners carry synephrine or other stimulants that raise cardiovascular load.

How to choose a supplement as a runner

  • Prioritise transparency: choose products that declare their doses (avoid proprietary blends).
  • Count your total daily caffeine including coffee, gels and pre-workouts.
  • Think about sleep: if you train in the evening, avoid stimulants or pick a caffeine-free option.
  • Be wary of diuretics: losing water is not losing fat, and it costs you hydration.
  • Test tolerance in training, never on race day.
  • If you're tested: look for independent batch certification (e.g. Informed Sport), knowing it reduces but does not eliminate the risk.
  • Don't exceed the manufacturer's dose and stop at any adverse symptom.

When NOT to use it: energy availability and RED-S

This is where fat burners can do real harm: when used to compensate for eating little and training a lot. When the energy left after training is insufficient over time (low energy availability), you can slip into RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport). The IOC consensus describes consequences for metabolism, hormonal and menstrual function, bone health, immunity, protein synthesis and mental health, with more stress fractures and more illness. Endurance sports are among the most exposed.

A runner should not use stimulants to paper over lack of sleep, an excessive deficit, poor recovery, training overload or insufficient nutrition. If that sounds like you, a fat burner won't fix it. We are not diagnosing: if warning signs appear (persistent fatigue, repeated injuries, menstrual changes), consult a sports physician or a dietitian.

Safety, contraindications and anti-doping

These supplements may not be suitable for: minors; pregnancy or breastfeeding; caffeine-sensitive people; those with cardiovascular problems or hypertension; anxiety or significant sleep disorders; anyone on medication; and athletes with relevant medical history. No supplement is "100% safe". When in doubt, consult a doctor or dietitian.

Anti-doping: even when the label ingredients are legal, supplements can be contaminated with prohibited substances not listed on the label; anti-doping bodies warn about this risk repeatedly. "No prohibited substances" on the label is not a guarantee. Higenamine, found in some fat burners, is prohibited by WADA; pharmaceutical diuretics too. Caffeine and synephrine are not prohibited, but are on WADA's Monitoring Program. A permitted ingredient does not equal a product free of contamination risk: look for independent batch certification where it exists, and check the current WADA Prohibited List.

Nutrition and training to lose fat without hurting performance

The strategy that works doesn't fit in a capsule:

  • A moderate, sustainable deficit, not aggressive, to protect recovery and performance.
  • Enough protein to preserve muscle in a deficit. See our protein guide for runners.
  • Strength training 2-3 days a week, on top of running.
  • Daily activity volume (NEAT): move more throughout the day.
  • Sleep and load management: poor sleep sabotages appetite and fat loss.
  • Adherence: the best plan is the one you can keep. A structured training plan helps you sustain it.

Frequently asked questions

Do fat burners actually work?

Not the way marketing sells them. None causes meaningful fat loss on its own; the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements considers the evidence for most weak. What drives fat loss is energy balance, protein, strength, activity volume, sleep and adherence. Some ingredients, especially caffeine, have a modest effect on energy or performance, not on fat.

What is the best fat burner for running?

For a runner, the most honest option is usually the simplest: caffeine in an adjustable dose. Among the ingredients in this comparison, it is the one with the most consistent evidence for improving specific aspects of performance; its effect on fat loss is small. If you prefer a thermogenic formula, HSN Evoripped and AMIX Burn & Dry declare most of their doses. The stimulant load and the effect on sleep matter more than the ingredient list.

Can you take it before training?

Many are taken 30-60 minutes before for their caffeine. The problem is the total with coffee, pre-workouts and gels: it's easy to exceed reference amounts (EFSA: ~400 mg/day, ~200 mg per dose). Test tolerance in training, never on race day.

Can I combine it with coffee or caffeinated gels?

Yes, but add up all your caffeine for the day. Many burners hide 150-300 mg per dose, plus synephrine or other stimulants. Overdoing it worsens sleep, and poor sleep sabotages exactly what drives fat loss.

Does it help lose belly fat?

There is no spot reduction. No supplement burns fat from a specific area; fat is lost globally with a sustained deficit, and where you lose it first depends on genetics.

Can it affect sleep?

Yes, through caffeine. EFSA notes that 100 mg near bedtime can increase how long it takes to fall asleep. Since sleep is key for recovery and appetite regulation, a burner that costs you sleep is counterproductive.

Is a formula with or without caffeine better?

It depends on you. Caffeine is the best-supported ingredient, but if you are sensitive, train at night or already have a lot, a stimulant-free option avoids interfering with sleep, though its evidence for fat loss is weak.

Are they suitable during race preparation?

Only with great caution. Aggressive weight loss during a block reduces energy availability and raises the risk of fatigue, injury and poor performance (RED-S). Don't use stimulants to compensate for lack of sleep or an excessive deficit. Never try a new supplement on race day.

Can a fat burner cause an anti-doping positive?

It's a real risk. Even when ingredients are legal, supplements can be contaminated with prohibited substances not listed on the label; anti-doping bodies warn about this risk repeatedly. Higenamine is prohibited by WADA and pharmaceutical diuretics too. A label saying "no prohibited substances" is not a guarantee. Look for independent batch certification.

How long can you use it?

There is no universal medical duration. Follow the label and don't exceed the manufacturer's dose. Tolerance develops to some of caffeine's effects, but there is no universal cycling protocol; many users take periodic breaks. Stop at any adverse symptom and consult a healthcare professional.

This is not a blind scientific trial but an editorial comparison.
