Half Marathon Pacing Strategy: Race Pace, Splits and How to Finish Strong

The gap between a good time and a bad day is not always training. It is almost always pacing. How to ration your effort across all 21 km, from the gun to the finish line.

I have watched runners with months of serious training end up walking at km 17. The reason is nearly always the same: they went out too fast over the opening kilometres, ran up an oxygen debt by halfway and had nothing left to hold the pace to the finish. Along with hydration, pacing is the variable that most decides how a half marathon ends.

The Most Effective Strategy: Even Pace vs Negative Split

Two big pacing strategies work in a half marathon. Which one suits you comes down to how much racing experience you have and how well you read your own body under pressure.

Even Pace (Steady Effort)

You run every kilometre at the same pace from start to finish. It is the most reliable option for mid-level runners, because it keeps lactate from piling up and makes the race easy to plan. The catch is the opening kilometres: adrenaline and the pack around you always push you to go out faster than planned.

Negative Split (Faster Second Half)

You run the second half of the race between 30 seconds and 2 minutes faster than the first. This is what high-level runners do, because it squeezes the most out of aerobic efficiency. It demands a very restrained first half, mental discipline and a good feel for your own body. Execute it well and passing other runners over the last 5 km is a hard feeling to beat.

Recommendation: For your first or second half marathon, go with even pace. From your third onwards, once you know your race-day sensations, try a negative split in a training race before you take it into a goal race.

The Four Blocks of a Half Marathon

Splitting the 21 km into four blocks with different objectives is the most practical way to execute a good race strategy.

Block 1 — Km 0 to 5: Hold Back and Measure

The most expensive mistake in the half marathon happens in this block. Adrenaline, the pack and the flat opening kilometres all invite you to go out fast. Resist.

  • Run 10 to 15 seconds per kilometre below your target pace
  • Find a group at your pace, or use your watch so you do not get dragged along
  • It should feel comfortable, almost too easy

Block 2 — Km 5 to 12: Target Pace

The cruising section. Your body is running aerobically now and muscle temperature is where you want it. This is where you should be exactly on target pace, or a touch below it.

  • Check your watch every 2 km and correct gently, never abruptly
  • Use the aid stations at km 5 and km 10 to drink
  • Keep your breathing rhythmic and relaxed

Block 3 — Km 12 to 18: The Danger Zone

This is where you find out whether the strategy was right. Glycogen stores start to drop, fatigue builds and your head starts negotiating. If you started well, this stretch is hard but manageable.

  • Pay less attention to the watch and more to your form
  • Take your planned gel or nutrition at km 14-15
  • If the pace slips, accept 5 sec/km slower and do not panic
  • Count the runners you pass: it is fuel if you started sensibly

Block 4 — Km 18 to 21.1: Push

The last 3 kilometres are a maximum effort. If you have reserves left, this is the moment to empty them. The distance is short enough now to be mentally manageable.

  • Raise your cadence, do not lengthen your stride: that is how you get hurt
  • Use the finish line or a visible landmark as a mental reference
  • The last kilometre is the one that matters: give it everything

Using Your GPS Watch on Race Day

Your GPS watch is the most valuable tool you have in a half marathon, but it can also turn into a source of anxiety if you do not know how to use it.

Which screens to set up

  • Current pace: averaged over the last 3-5 seconds, not instant
  • Lap pace: the average pace for the kilometre you are running
  • Heart rate: especially useful on hills and in heat
  • Total distance: so you always know exactly where you are

The GPS problem on city courses

In cities with tall buildings, GPS can lose up to 50-100 metres of accuracy per kilometre. If your watch beeps km 1 before you pass the official marker, the pace reading is probably a little distorted. The official markers painted on the road are always more reliable than your GPS kilometres.

Handling Aid Stations

A typical half marathon has aid stations at km 5, 10, 15 and 20. Decide in advance how you are going to handle each one.

  • Km 5: Drink even if you are not thirsty. At fast paces you can run straight through, but for target times slower than 1:45 it is worth taking something.
  • Km 10: Drinking here is non-negotiable. It is the most important station, because you are at halfway with your hydration still in good shape.
  • Km 15: Drink, and consider taking a gel if you are carrying one. Km 14-18 is the most demanding stretch.
  • Km 20: Almost done. Drink if you need to, but do not come to a stop.
How to drink without losing pace: Pinch the plastic cup roughly in half to create a channel. You get far more control and spill less. Some runners prefer to walk for 8-10 seconds: what it gives you in hydration is worth what it costs in time.

Common Pacing Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Explosive start: The first 500 metres, with the pack and the adrenaline, pull your body into a pace it cannot hold. Check your watch at km 1, and if you are more than 20 seconds per kilometre faster than your target, ease off.
Mistake 2 — Following someone whose level you do not know: Latching onto a runner who looks like they are going your pace over the first kilometres can end badly if that person is quicker than you. Trust your watch, not the runner in front of you.
Mistake 3 — Ignoring the hills: On a course with climbs, holding the same GPS pace uphill as on the flat means multiplying the effort. On climbs, hold the effort (heart rate or RPE) and let the pace drop slightly.
Mistake 4 — Having no plan B: If race day turns out hot, your original target pace may be too ambitious. Every degree above 18-20°C can cost between 5 and 15 seconds per kilometre depending on the runner's level.

Frequently Asked Questions About Half Marathon Pacing

Even pace or negative split: which is better in a half marathon?

For most runners, even pace is safer and produces better results. A negative split is ideal if you have experience and self-control, but it needs a very conservative first half, which can be hard to execute with start-line adrenaline.

How much slower than target pace should I start?

Between 10 and 15 seconds per kilometre slower than your target pace for the first 5 km. That absorbs the adrenaline of the start and lets you speed up with something in reserve in the second half.

Why is km 14-18 so dangerous?

It is the stretch where glycogen stores run out if you went off too hard, the body starts to fatigue and motivation drops. If you reach it with your pace under control and good hydration, it is manageable. If you started fast, it is where a lot of runners fall apart.

Should I watch the pace on my watch or go by perceived effort?

Both. The GPS watch gives you objectivity, but perceived effort is the real signal from your body. On hills and in heat, GPS pace misleads you: use heart rate as a secondary reference.

How do I handle aid stations without losing much time?

Decide in advance which kilometres you will drink at and whether you will walk. Walking for 8-10 seconds to drink properly costs under 15 seconds in total and can save your last 5 km. Use the folded-cup technique to drink on the move.

What happens if I go out too fast in the first few kilometres?

Your body builds an oxygen and lactate debt that you pay back with interest between km 15 and 21. It is the most common mistake and it can cost you 5 to 20 minutes compared with a well-executed even pace.

Should I use my GPS watch for the whole race?

Yes, but do not obsess over it. Check your pace every 1-2 km to make gentle corrections. Looking at your watch every stride creates anxiety and pulls your attention away from your running form and from how you actually feel.

How do I know whether my goal time is realistic?

Use your training pace on long runs: if you run 18-19 km comfortably at 5:30/km, a realistic target is around 1:58-2:00. Subtract 5 to 10 seconds per km from your comfortable training pace to estimate race pace.
