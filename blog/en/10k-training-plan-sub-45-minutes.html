Sub 45 10K Training Plan: 10 Weeks to the Next Level

Running 10K under 45 minutes is the mark that separates the recreational runner from the competitive one. This 10-week plan gets you there.

Sub-45 in the 10K: What It Takes

The sub-45 is not simply running faster — it is a different level of fitness. Before you start this plan, assess honestly whether you meet the requirements:

  • Goal pace: 4:30 min/km for 10 km
  • Minimum requirement: a 10K already run in under 50 minutes, or a 5K in under 22 minutes
  • Context: the sub-45 puts you in the top 15% of runners at road races in Spain
  • Experience needed: at least 2 years of regular running, with previous interval training experience
Warning: This plan includes demanding quality sessions that are not suitable for runners who have never done intervals. If you have not yet worked through an entry-level 10K plan, do that first. Progressing in the right order cuts the injury risk in half.

The Full 10-Week Plan

The plan follows a structure of two loading weeks followed by one deload week. That rhythm lets the body absorb the stimulus and reach the hard weeks recovered. Never skip the deload weeks — they are part of the training.

1 6x600m at 4:15, 75s rec. 40 min Z2 14 km easy 45 km
2 5x1000m at 4:20, 2 min rec. 40 min with fartlek 6x1 min at 4:00 15 km easy 50 km
3 20 min tempo at 4:30 + 4x400m at 4:00 40 min easy 14 km progressive 50 km
4 DELOAD 4x600m controlled 30 min easy 10 km 35 km
5 8x600m at 4:12, 75s rec. 45 min with 3x10 min at 4:30 16 km easy 55 km
6 4x1500m at 4:18, 2 min 30s rec. 40 min easy 16 km with 5 km at 4:30 56 km
7 25 min tempo at 4:28 + 6x400m at 4:00 45 min easy 17 km easy 58 km

About the taper week: Week 10 drops the volume to 50-60% but keeps the intensity in the single quality session. The goal is to reach the race rested without losing muscular sharpness. Do not add extra workouts just because you feel fresh.

The Paces in the Plan

Respecting the pace zones is the difference between a plan that works and one that injures you. The most common mistake among intermediate runners is running the easy days too fast, which makes it impossible to give the right effort in the quality sessions.

Zone Pace Description
Z2 easy 5:30 - 6:00 /km 80% of total volume. Conversational, always.
Tempo / threshold 4:25 - 4:35 /km Race pace ±5 sec. Uncomfortable but sustainable.
Medium intervals 4:15 - 4:25 /km 1000-1500 m reps. Hard but controlled.
Short intervals 4:00 - 4:12 /km 400-600 m reps. High intensity with full recovery.

Key Workouts

The 1000m Reps at 4:20 min/km

The hardest session in the plan, and the one that changes the most. Four or five reps of 1 km at 10-12 seconds per kilometre faster than goal pace. The 2-minute recovery between reps is mandatory — do not shorten it, even if you feel you could.

The physiological reason: 1000m reps raise VO2max more efficiently than any other distance in this pace range. If you want to dig into how these sessions are built, read our guide to interval and fartlek training.

The 25-Minute Tempo at Goal Pace

This simulates the middle 25 minutes of a 10K race. Being able to hold 4:30 min/km cold for 25 continuous minutes is the best confirmation that you are ready for the sub-45. If you get to 25 minutes comfortably in the week 7 tempo, the race is yours.

How to run it: 15 minutes of easy warm-up + 25 minutes at 4:28-4:30 min/km + 10 minutes of cool-down. Do not start the tempo faster than goal pace, even if it feels easy. If you want the theory behind this effort, see our article on threshold pace in running.

The Long Run with a Progressive Finish (16-17 km)

The Saturday long runs in weeks 6 and 9 finish with the last 5 km at goal pace (4:30 min/km). This is the workout that builds specific endurance: the ability to run fast when the legs already carry load. Start the first 11-12 km at easy pace (5:30-6:00 min/km) and bring the pace down gradually over the closing kilometres.

Race-Day Strategy

The sub-45 is won or lost in the pace of the first 2 km. The atmosphere of a road race makes those opening metres feel easy at 4:15 min/km. That is the surest route to a disaster at km 8.

  • km 1: 4:40 min/km — do not fly off the line, whatever the crowd does to you
  • km 2-7: 4:28-4:30 min/km — steady, controlled goal pace
  • km 8-10: if you feel good, drop to 4:20-4:25 min/km
  • 5 km split: aim for 22:30-22:45 to leave yourself margin in the second half
A useful tactic: In the first 2 km, let the runners who go out faster pass you. It is hard mentally, but it is exactly the right thing to do. You will see them again at km 8, going the other way.

Supporting Strength Work for the Sub-45

At this level, strength training is no longer optional. Two 20-30 minute sessions a week improve running economy and significantly reduce the risk of overuse injuries.

Essential exercises for intermediate runners:

  • Squats, bodyweight or barbell: 3x12 reps. The foundation of the whole posterior chain.
  • Lunges: 3x10 per leg. They improve stability on foot strike.
  • Heel raises: 3x20. They protect against plantar fasciitis and Achilles problems.
  • Glute bridges: 3x15. The glute is the main engine of your stride at sub-45 pace.
  • Side planks: 3x30 seconds. They stabilise the pelvis and stop it collapsing sideways when you tire.
When to do strength work: Your running rest days (Monday, Wednesday) are the best moment. Never the day before an interval session — your legs need to be fresh to hit the right paces.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Sub-45 10K Plan

How much experience do I need to go for the sub-45?

At least 2 years of regular running and a 10K already completed in under 50 minutes. Going from 50 to 45 minutes takes a solid aerobic base and previous experience with quality training.

Can I follow this plan while training for a half marathon?

Not recommended. Each plan needs its own attention in terms of intensity and volume targets. Finish this plan first, take 2-3 weeks off racing and then start half marathon prep.

Are 16-17 km long runs really necessary for a 10K race?

Yes. Aerobic base volume is the platform that supports the intensity of the intervals. Without long runs, quality sessions do not produce the expected adaptations because the energy system is not developed enough.

What if I can only train 2 days in a given week?

Prioritise Tuesday's quality session and Saturday's long run. Thursday is the one you can drop occasionally. A two-day week does not ruin the plan if it happens 1-2 times across the 10 weeks.

How much can I improve starting from 50 minutes?

With this plan executed well, typical improvements are 5-7 minutes. The sub-45 is entirely realistic if you start from 48-50 minutes. If you start from 52-54 minutes, the sub-45 may be a target for a second training cycle.

Do I need to run intervals if I have never done them before?

If you have never run intervals, start with an entry-level 10K block first. The intervals in this plan are demanding and the body needs several weeks of adaptation to handle them without injury risk.

What GPS watch is enough to follow this plan?

Any watch with GPS and a configurable pace alert: Garmin Forerunner 165, Polar Pacer or COROS Pace 3. The interval paces need real-time feedback to be accurate.

Is it possible to break 45 minutes without a coach?

Yes. This plan is detailed enough to follow on your own. That said, a coach can adjust the load to your individual response to training, which optimises results and reduces the risk of overtraining.
