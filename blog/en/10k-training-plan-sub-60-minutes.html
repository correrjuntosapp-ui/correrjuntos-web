Sub 60 10K Training Plan: 8 Weeks to Break the Hour

The 60-minute barrier in the 10K is the first real goal for thousands of runners. This 8-week plan gives you the tools to cross it.

Breaking 60 minutes in the 10K is the goal that marks the step from casual jogger to proper runner. The pace you need is 6:00 min/km for 10 km — demanding, but completely within reach after 8 weeks of consistent work. This plan is built for someone who already runs but has not yet crossed that barrier.

Are You Ready to Go Sub-60 in the 10K?

Before you start, check the minimum requirement: you can run 8 km without stopping, even if that means 7:00-7:30 min/km. If you are not there yet, complete the free 0-5K plan first and come back afterwards.

  • Target pace: exactly 6:00 min/km for 10 km
  • Prerequisite: run 8 km non-stop, even at 7:00-7:30 min/km
  • Context: going sub-60 puts you at intermediate level — you are running faster than the average finisher at road races in Spain
  • Minimum experience: at least 3-4 months of regular running behind you
Level check: If your best 5K sits between 28 and 32 minutes, you are in the right range to attack the sub-60 in the 10K. Use that as your reference if you have never raced an official 10K.

The Full 8-Week Plan

The plan is built around three compulsory training days (Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday) plus an optional Sunday run in the highest-volume weeks. Monday, Wednesday and Friday are rest days or easy activity (stretching, easy cycling, walking).

Week Tuesday Thursday Saturday (long run) Sun. (optional) Total km
1 30 min easy 5x400m at 5:40, 90s rec. 8 km easy — 28 km
2 35 min easy with 3x5 min at 6:00 5x600m at 5:45 10 km easy — 32 km
3 4x1 km at 6:00, 2 min rec. 35 min easy 10 km with 3 km at 6:00 — 35 km
4 RECOVERY 3x800m easy 25 min easy 7 km — 22 km
5 5x800m at 5:50, 90s rec. 40 min easy 11 km easy 30 min easy 40 km
6 20 min at 6:00 + 5x400m at 5:35 35 min easy 12 km with 4 km at 6:00 — 42 km
7 3x2 km at 6:00, 2 min rec. 35 min easy 10 km progressive — 40 km
8 TAPER 3x1 km at target pace 20 min easy 5 km easy RACE 25 km + 10K
A note on recoveries: Every recovery between reps is a very easy jog or a walk. Never come to a complete stop — staying in motion helps clear lactate and gets the body ready for the next rep.

The Reference Paces for This Plan

Every type of session has its own pace. Respecting these zones matters as much as completing the workouts. Running your easy days too fast is the most common mistake in the plan, and the one that causes the most injuries.

Easy / Z2 6:45 - 7:30 /km Easy runs (80% of total volume)
Tempo / threshold 6:00 - 6:20 /km Continuous tempo sessions

The Key Sessions

Three types of session inside the plan account for most of the improvement. Understanding what each one is for will help you execute it properly.

The 800m Intervals

The most important session in the plan. The correct structure is: 10 minutes of easy jogging to warm up + the reps with their recoveries + 10 minutes of easy jogging to cool down. The key is the recovery between reps: walk or jog very easy — do not stop. Stopping cuts off the physiological stimulus the session is built around.

The 800m reps at 5:50 min/km are 10 seconds per kilometre faster than target pace. That margin is enough to drive adaptation without overloading the system. When the reps start to feel comfortable (week 5-6), that is the sign you are progressing.

The 20-Minute Tempo

20 continuous minutes at exactly 6:00 min/km. It is harder mentally than physically — holding the same pace without drifting takes concentration. This session teaches you what target pace actually feels like over a sustained block. It shows up in week 6, once the body has the base needed to hold it.

One practical tip: set a pace alert on your watch instead of staring at the screen. The goal is to internalise the pace, not to depend on the watch.

The Long Run with a Fast Finish

The Saturday long runs in weeks 3 and 6 include closing kilometres at target pace. The idea is to train the body to run fast when it is already tired, which is exactly what happens between kilometres 8 and 10 on race day. Ease into those kilometres progressively, not abruptly.

Race-Day Strategy

Executing the training plan well is half the job. The other half is racing with your head. How you spread the effort across the 10 km decides the result.

  • km 1-2: 6:10-6:15 min/km (start conservative, let the adrenaline settle)
  • km 3-7: exactly 6:00 min/km (cruise phase, steady pace)
  • km 8-10: let it go if the legs respond, 5:50-5:55 if everything feels good
  • 5 km split: aim for roughly 30:00-30:15
The classic mistake: 80% of the runners who miss the sub-60 lose it in the first 2 km by going out too hard. Race adrenaline makes those opening kilometres feel comfortable at 5:45 min/km when they should be at 6:10. You pay the bill between km 7 and km 10.

One tactic that works: find a runner at your level in the opening kilometres and use their pace as a reference. If they are running 6:00 and you feel good, hold that position. Do not pass anyone until km 7.

Nutrition for a 10K Race

The 10K is a short distance in fuelling terms. You do not need energy gels or a complex nutrition strategy. What you do need is to avoid the basic mistakes on race day.

  • The night before: a normal dinner, no changes. Pasta, rice or similar if you want, but do not stuff yourself thinking you need to carb-load for 10 km.
  • Race day (2 hours before): banana + toast with olive oil or jam + coffee if you normally drink it.
  • Hydration: drink water as usual, not in excess. A glass 30 minutes before is enough.
  • Gels: not necessary for a sub-60 10K. If your time is going to be over 60-65 minutes, a gel at km 5 can help, but it is not essential.
José's tip: Most in-race stomach problems come from trying new foods on race day. Eat exactly what you eat before your important training sessions. Race day is not the day for experiments.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Sub-60 10K Plan

Can I follow this plan if I have never raced a 10K?

Yes, as long as you already run 8 km non-stop. This plan gets you ready to race your first 10K with the goal of breaking 60 minutes. Plenty of runners complete their first official 10K and go sub-60 at the same time on this plan.

How many days a week do I need to train?

Three days minimum (Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday). Sunday is optional but worth adding in weeks 5 to 7 to build a little extra volume and reinforce your aerobic base.

Is it normal for the 800m intervals to feel brutal in the first weeks?

Completely normal. The first 2-3 interval sessions hurt. By week 3-4 the body adapts and they start to feel manageable. If they are still a fight in week 5, drop the pace by 5 sec/km until your body absorbs the stimulus.

What if I cannot hit the interval paces?

Slow down by 5-10 seconds per kilometre and finish the session. Completing the reps matters far more than the exact pace in the early weeks. Your body needs time to adapt, and the paces in the plan are targets, not obligations.

Do I need a GPS watch for this plan?

Strongly recommended. Controlling your pace during intervals is much harder without one. A Garmin Forerunner 55 or similar is more than enough for this plan and it is an investment that will last for years.

Can I follow the plan if I work and only have mornings free?

Yes, the plan works perfectly with morning training. Just make sure you eat something 30-45 minutes before the interval sessions so you have energy available. For easy runs, running fasted is a valid option.

Which 10K should I target after this plan?

Any local road race with a flat course. Avoid big elevation changes or twisty routes for your first attempt at the time. Check your area's race calendar — in spring and autumn there are options in almost every city.

Can I move on to the sub-45 plan once I break 60?

Yes, but take 3-4 weeks off racing and hard training before starting the next plan. Your body needs to recover from the training cycle before it can absorb a tougher stimulus. The sub-45 plan is the natural next step once you have rested.
