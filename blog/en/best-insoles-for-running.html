When do you actually need running insoles?

Before spending money on insoles, answer three questions:

  1. Do you get recurring pain in the sole of your foot, heel, knee or shin? If you do, and the pain is linked to running, an insole can help unload the area.
  2. Do your shoes have more than 600-700 km on them? The original insole flattens before the outsole does. You can be running on dead foam without knowing it. A new insole gives you cushioning back without buying new shoes.
  3. Has your podiatrist or physio told you that you have marked overpronation, a flat foot or a high arch? That is where an arch-support insole goes from "comfort" to active prevention.
What an insole does not do: it does not fix bad running form, it does not replace ankle and hip strength work, and it does not cure an active injury without rest. If you have sharp pain, see a physio first. An insole is a support tool, not a magic remedy.
One simple indicator: look at the outsole of your current shoes. If the wear is asymmetric (more on the inside = overpronation, more on the outside = supination), an arch-support insole can compensate for that tendency and reduce the risk of overload injuries.

Types of insole: gel, semi-rigid, structured

Not all running insoles work the same way. There are three categories you should know before choosing:

1. Gel or foam insoles (pure cushioning)

Examples: Sorbothane Full Strike, Scholl GelActiv Running, riemot Sport. They absorb ground impact but have little or no biomechanical action. They are the cheapest (€15-€20) and the right fit for neutral runners who just want more comfort or want to delay shoe wear. They do not correct your gait.

2. Semi-rigid insoles with arch support

Examples: Sidas 3D Run Protect, Sidas Run 3Feet Protect Mid, Dr. Scholl's Performance. They combine a layer of foam or gel with a support plate that influences how weight is distributed. They are the sweet spot for most runners: €25-€40, real biomechanical action, compatible with almost any shoe.

3. Structured / orthotic insoles

Examples: Superfeet Run, currexSole RunPro, PowerStep Pinnacle. Deep heel cup, rigid or semi-rigid structure that actively controls overpronation. These influence running mechanics the most and last the longest (800-1,000 km). They are the ones indicated for runners with overpronation, chronic fasciitis or repeat injuries. €35-€50.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need insoles if I already have good running shoes?

It depends. If you run pain-free, your shoes suit your gait and you have no history of recurring injuries, you probably don't need them. Insoles are useful when you have marked overpronation, plantar fasciitis, knee pain linked to alignment, a pronounced flat or high arch, or when the original insole in your favourite shoe is no longer in good shape. They are not a universal fix: if you are unsure, a sports podiatrist can tell you in 30 minutes whether you need them.

How much do good running insoles cost?

On Amazon Spain the useful range sits between €15 and €50. Entry-level options (Sorbothane, Scholl GelActiv Running, riemot) cost €15-€20 and work for runners with no structural issues. Mid-range models (Sidas 3D Run Protect, Superfeet Run) cost €30-€40 and add real anatomical support. Premium ones (currexSole RunPro, PowerStep Pinnacle) cost €40-€50. Custom podiatrist insoles cost €80-€200 and are the definitive option for complex cases.

Do insoles help with plantar fasciitis?

Yes, they are one of the most effective conservative treatments for plantar fasciitis. Insoles with a deep heel cup and arch support unload the fascia and reduce pain, especially on that first step in the morning. For plantar fasciitis the best fits are the Superfeet Run (structured heel cup), the Sidas 3D Run Protect (heel gel) and the currexSole RunPro (dynamic DAT arch support). Combine them with Achilles tendon and plantar muscle stretching.

How do I know if I overpronate, supinate or have a neutral gait?

Look at the outsole of your current shoes: wear on the inside of the heel and the arch area means you overpronate. Wear on the outside means you supinate. Even wear means you are neutral. You can also do the wet foot test: wet the sole of your foot and step on paper. If you see almost the whole footprint (a filled-in arch), you have a flat foot and probably overpronate. If the arch is very marked, you have a high arch and possibly supinate. For a precise answer, visit a sports podiatrist or a running shop with free gait analysis. If you already wear shoes for overpronators, pairing them with an insole like the currexSole multiplies the effect.

How long do running insoles last?

Generic gel insoles (Scholl, Sorbothane) last between 400 and 600 km before losing their cushioning. Insoles with a rigid structure (Superfeet, currexSole) hold up for 800-1,000 km because they don't rely on foam alone. The sign that you need to replace them: the gel is flattened, the heel cup has lost its shape, or the pain they had relieved comes back. Simple rule: change them when you change shoes.

Can I use insoles in any running shoe?

Almost always yes, but you have to remove the original insole before putting the new one in so you don't lose the fit. Minimalist or zero-drop shoes have so little internal volume that some insoles simply won't fit. Maximalist shoes (Hoka, Brooks, ASICS Nimbus) have more room and take thicker insoles better. If the insole tightens the fit, you may need to go half a size up.

Is a gel insole better than a rigid one?

It depends on the goal. Gel insoles absorb impact but have little biomechanical action: they cushion well but don't correct your gait. Semi-rigid or structured ones (Superfeet, currexSole, Sidas 3Feet) influence the mechanics of your footstrike, control overpronation and actively support the arch. For neutral runners who just want more comfort: gel. For runners with overpronation, fasciitis or knee pain from poor foot strike: semi-rigid or structured.
