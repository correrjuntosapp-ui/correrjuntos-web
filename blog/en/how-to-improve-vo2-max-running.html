VO2 Max in Running: What It Is and How to Improve It in 2026

Everything you need to know about the single most important marker of your aerobic fitness. Workouts, reference tables and a 6-week plan. Updated May 2026.

If you have been running for a while, you have almost certainly come across VO2 max. Maybe you have seen it on your Garmin or on your Apple Watch screen. A number that goes up and down and that supposedly says a lot about your fitness. But what is it exactly? And how do you actually improve it?

I still remember the first time I paid any attention to my own VO2 max, years ago. I saw a 42 on my watch and had no idea whether that was good, bad or somewhere in between. What I do know is that once I understood what it meant and how to train it, my performance took a jump that years of aimless mileage had never given me. Today I want to walk you through everything I have learned about this metric in 20 years of running, in plain language and with workouts you can start this week.

What is VO2 max?

VO2 max (maximal oxygen uptake) is the largest amount of oxygen your body can take in, transport and use during maximal-intensity exercise. It is measured in millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute (ml/kg/min).

In practical terms, VO2 max reflects how well your cardiovascular system (heart, lungs, blood) and your muscles work together under maximal effort. The higher your VO2 max, the more oxygen reaches your muscles and the more energy they can produce aerobically.

Think of it this way: VO2 max is like the engine size of your car. Two cars can both cruise at 120 km/h, but the one with the bigger engine does it with less effort. The same applies to runners: a high VO2 max lets you run faster with less fatigue.

VO2 max has a significant genetic component (estimated at 20-50%), but the good news is that the trainable share is huge. With the right stimulus, any runner can improve their VO2 max meaningfully, whatever their starting point. If you are a beginner runner, the gains can come especially fast.

Why does it matter so much for runners?

VO2 max is the best predictor of endurance race performance there is. It is not the only factor — running economy, lactate threshold and mental strength count too — but it is the foundation everything else is built on.

Its importance to you as a runner comes down to three key points:

  • Performance predictor: there is a direct correlation between VO2 max and your times over 5K, 10K, half marathon and marathon. The higher your VO2 max, the faster you can run at any given percentage of your aerobic capacity.
  • More efficiency at easy paces: when your VO2 max goes up, your usual pace (5:30/km, say) becomes a smaller percentage of your maximum capacity. The result: that same pace feels more comfortable, with a lower heart rate and less fatigue.
  • Cardiovascular health marker: beyond athletic performance, VO2 max is one of the best predictors of longevity. Recent studies have shown that a high VO2 max is associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and even all-cause mortality.

In short: improving your VO2 max makes you faster, more resilient and healthier. It is one of the highest-return investments you can make in your training.

How to measure your VO2 max

There are three main ways to find out your VO2 max, each with its own advantages:

1. Lab test (the gold standard)

This is the most accurate measurement. It is done at sports medicine centres on a treadmill or cycle ergometer, with a mask that analyses your respiratory gases and continuous heart rate monitoring. The protocol raises the intensity step by step until you reach your limit. Price: between 100 and 200 euros. Ideal if you want an exact figure to plan your training seriously.

2. Cooper test (field test)

The Cooper test is the most reliable free alternative. You run as far as you can in 12 minutes on flat ground — ideally an athletics track. Then you take the distance covered and apply this formula:

Cooper test formula: VO2 max = (distance in metres - 504.9) / 44.73. For example, if you cover 2,800 m in 12 minutes: VO2 max = (2800 - 504.9) / 44.73 = 51.3 ml/kg/min.

3. GPS watches and wearables

Devices from Garmin, Apple Watch, COROS or Polar estimate your VO2 max from heart rate and running pace data. They are not as accurate as a lab test — the margin of error can be 3-5 ml/kg/min — but they are excellent for tracking your trend over time. Practical tip: The Cooper test is free and surprisingly accurate. Do one every 6-8 weeks to measure your real progress. Always compare under the same conditions: same track, same time of day, same warm-up.

5 workouts to improve your VO2 max

This is where the good stuff starts. After years of trying different methods, these are the five workouts that have the biggest impact on your VO2 max:

1. Classic intervals (the king)

5 x 3 minutes at 90-95% of your maximum heart rate, with 2 minutes of recovery at an easy jog. This is the most science-backed stimulus for improving VO2 max. The key is that each 3-minute rep keeps you close to your maximal oxygen uptake for long enough to count.

2. 1000-metre reps

4-6 x 1000 m at your 5K pace, with 400 m of jogging as recovery. A classic track session that pairs the VO2 max stimulus with specific race speed. Perfect for runners who also want to lower their race times.

3. VO2 fartlek (30-30)

30 seconds fast / 30 seconds jog, repeated 15-20 times. The fast phase should be at your 3K-5K pace. This Scandinavian format is brutally effective because oxygen uptake never drops far enough during the slow phase, so the VO2 max stimulus builds up across the session. Ideal if long intervals bore you.

4. Hill reps

8-10 x 60-90 seconds running hard uphill (9/10 effort), jogging back down as recovery. Hills add a strength component to the VO2 max stimulus. The gradient also cuts the joint impact compared with flat intervals, which makes them an excellent option for injury-prone runners.

5. Progressive tempo

20 continuous minutes starting in zone 3 (comfortable-moderate pace) and finishing in zone 4-5 (hard effort) over the final 5 minutes. This session trains lactate threshold and VO2 max at the same time, and teaches your body to handle building fatigue — something that matters enormously on race day.

Common mistakes when training VO2 max

After years of training and watching other runners train, these are the mistakes that come up again and again when someone sets out to improve their VO2 max:

  1. Running every session hard. The 80/20 rule exists for a reason: 80% of your weekly volume should be in zone 2 (easy, conversational) and only 20% at high intensity. Train hard every day and you pile up fatigue without leaving room for adaptation. More is not better.
  2. Not taking enough recovery between reps. If the rests are too short, you cannot hold the intensity you need on the following reps. If they are too long, you lose the cumulative effect. The guideline of 2-3 minutes of easy jogging between 3-5 minute reps is the best supported.
  3. Obsessing over the number on your watch. The VO2 max estimates from GPS watches shift with temperature, hydration, sleep and dozens of other factors. Do not get frustrated if it drops 1-2 points from one day to the next. Watch the trend across weeks and months, not the daily spikes.
  4. Ignoring your aerobic base. VO2 max intervals only work if there is a solid aerobic base underneath them. If you cannot run 45-60 minutes comfortably in zone 2, building that base should be your priority before adding high-intensity sessions.
  5. Expecting results in 1-2 weeks. The physiological adaptations behind a higher VO2 max — increased blood volume, greater mitochondrial density, better muscle capillarisation — need a minimum of 6-8 weeks of consistent stimulus. Patience is part of the training.

Frequently asked questions

What is VO2 max and why does it matter in running?

VO2 max is the maximum amount of oxygen your body can use during intense exercise, measured in ml/kg/min. It is the best indicator of your aerobic capacity and your performance in distance races. The higher your VO2 max, the faster you can run for longer.

What is a good VO2 max for a runner?

It depends on age and sex. For men aged 30-39, a VO2 max of 40-45 is average, 46-54 is good and above 55 is excellent. For women of the same age, 35-40 is average, 41-49 good and above 50 excellent. Elite runners go beyond 70-80 ml/kg/min.

How can I measure my VO2 max?

There are three ways: a lab test (the most accurate, using a gas-analysis mask), a field test such as the Cooper test (running as far as you can in 12 minutes), or a GPS watch such as a Garmin or Apple Watch, which estimates it from heart rate and pace data.

How long does it take to improve VO2 max?

With consistent interval training 2-3 times a week, you can see improvements of 5-15% in 6 to 8 weeks. Beginners will improve faster than experienced runners. Consistency matters more than intensity.

Are intervals the best workout for improving VO2 max?

Yes, intervals at 90-95% of your maximum heart rate are the most effective stimulus for improving VO2 max. Reps of 3-5 minutes with 2-3 minutes of recovery are the format best supported by the science. Pair them with long easy runs in zone 2 for the best results.
