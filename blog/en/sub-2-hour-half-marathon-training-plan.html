Sub 2 Hour Half Marathon Training Plan: 12 Weeks to Break the Barrier

Two hours is the number thousands of amateur runners chase. This plan gives you the tools to cross it. Updated May 2026.

Breaking two hours in the half marathon means covering 21.0975km at exactly 5:41 per kilometre for 1 hour, 59 minutes and 59 seconds. That number is not round by accident — it is a psychological threshold that splits thousands of runners into those who did it and those who came close. This 12-week plan is built to put you in the first group.

Are You Ready for Sub-2?

Before you start, check that you meet the minimum requirements. Sub-2 is not a beginner goal: it takes a solid aerobic base and some race experience over distance.

Minimum requirement: you have finished a full half marathon, or you can run 10km in under 52 minutes. If you are not at that starting point yet, build with a base plan first before attempting sub-2.

Quick test: Go out and run 10km at a steady effort. Finish under 52:00 and you have the aerobic base for this plan. Take longer than 54:00 and you should spend another 8 weeks building base before you start.

The Training Blocks of the Plan

The plan splits into 4 progressive blocks. Each one has a specific job and you should not skip any of them, even if you feel great at the end of a block and want to jump ahead.

Weeks 1-4: Aerobic Base

The goal here is to build without getting hurt. Volume is moderate, paces are easy and the quality sessions are short. It looks undemanding, but you are preparing tendons, ligaments and the cardiovascular system for what comes next. Do not underestimate this phase.

Weeks 5-8: Intensity and Speed

This is where the real work starts. Reps get longer, tempos move towards the 5:41/km target pace and long runs go from 15 to 18km. Your body learns to run at race pace and to hold it for longer and longer stretches. It is the hardest phase of the plan.

Weeks 9-11: Peak and Race Rehearsal

The highest-volume, highest-intensity weeks. In week 10 you run the most important rehearsal of the block: 20km with the last 5km at target pace. That session tells you whether you are ready for race day. Week 11 is a down week before the taper.

Week 12: Tapering

Cut volume to 50-60% but keep small doses of intensity so you do not lose sharpness. Rest properly over the final 3 days. A taper done right can be worth 2-3 minutes on race day.

José's tip: On down weeks (W4, W8, W11) you will probably feel fresh enough to do more. Resist it. Down weeks are where the body absorbs the previous load and comes back stronger. Skipping them is the single most common mistake that ends in an injury right before the race.

Your Training Paces by Zone

80% of your training should be easy. The remaining 20% should be genuinely hard. That split, known as polarised training distribution, underpins elite training plans and works exactly the same way for recreational runners. Here are the reference paces for someone targeting 2h00:00 in the half marathon:

The Key Sessions for Sub-2

The 25-Minute Tempo at 5:41/km

This is the most important session in the whole plan. You hold exact race pace for 25 uninterrupted minutes. The full structure is: 10 minutes easy warm-up, 25 minutes at 5:41/km without stopping, 10 minutes cool-down.

If you cannot hold the 25 minutes unbroken in the early weeks, that is fine: do 3 reps of 8 minutes with 2 minutes of recovery between them. Shave the recovery down week by week until you can run the 25 minutes straight through.

The 1000m Intervals

Target pace for the 1000m reps is 5:05-5:10/km, which is 10 to 15 seconds per kilometre faster than race pace. Recovery between reps should be 90 seconds of easy jogging. Never come to a complete stop during the recovery — keep moving.

Important: Never do more than 6-8 reps in a session, however good you feel. Piling on extra interval volume is the leading cause of patellar tendinitis in half marathon runners.

The Long Run with a Progression

The last 20-30% of the long run should be at target race pace. In week 10 that means 15km easy (Z2) + 5km at 5:41/km. This session teaches the body to run at goal pace on already-fatigued legs — exactly what happens between km 15 and 21 on race day.

Race-Day Strategy

Average target pace is 5:41/km, but you do not run the race at a flat pace. Plan the distribution like this:

  • Km 1-5: 5:50/km. Conservative start. The crowd, the buzz and the adrenaline all push you to go out too fast. Resist. You will win those 9 seconds per km back with interest in the second half.
  • Km 6-15: 5:38-5:41/km. Target pace, held steady. Settle in, breathe rhythmically and ignore the runners going past you. Trust the training.
  • Km 16-21: 5:30/km if the legs respond. If you have managed your energy well, this is where you can lift slightly. If not, hold 5:41/km and the job is still done.
Warning: Going out at 5:20/km for the first 5km will cost you between 8 and 12 minutes over the last 6km. Muscle glycogen runs out sooner and the wall arrives early. Do not run on feel in the first half: trust the pace on your watch.

Nutrition for Sub-2

A sub-2 half marathon means roughly 90 minutes of sustained maximum effort. Over that kind of duration the body needs between 60 and 90 grams of carbohydrate per hour to keep performing.

Gel strategy during the race

Take 2-3 gels during the race on this schedule: the first at km 6 (before glycogen starts dropping), the second at km 12 (topping the system back up), and an optional third at km 17 if you need it for the final push.

Product suggestions: GU Energy Gel (caffeine-free versions for the early gels, and a 40mg caffeine one for the last) or Maurten 100 (easier on the stomach, and pricier). The essential part is training with the same gels you will race with — test your stomach on the long runs.

Golden rule: Never try a gel on race day that you have not taken in training. Gastrointestinal trouble is the number 1 cause of DNFs in half marathons. Test everything on your long runs.
