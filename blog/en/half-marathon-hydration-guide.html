Half Marathon Hydration: How Much to Drink and When

Dehydration starts costing you performance at 2% of body weight lost. Here is the practical way to hydrate before, during and after your 21K.

Hydration is one of the biggest levers on half marathon performance, and one of the most improvised. I have seen perfectly trained runners give away several minutes in the closing kilometres simply because they did not drink enough — or because they drank too much of the wrong thing. This guide covers everything you need to reach the finish line properly hydrated.

Why Hydration Matters in the Half Marathon

The human body is roughly 60% water. During hard exercise that balance shifts quickly through sweat. In normal temperatures a runner loses between 400 and 800ml of fluid per hour. In hot or humid conditions that figure can go beyond 1,200ml per hour.

The direct consequence of not replacing that fluid is a drop in blood volume, which forces the heart to work harder to move oxygen to the muscles. Once fluid loss passes 2% of body weight — only 1.4 litres for a 70 kg runner — performance falls by 5 to 10%. In a half marathon that can mean 5-10 extra minutes.

The good news is that a hydration strategy is fairly easy to execute well. You just have to plan it before the race instead of improvising at the aid stations.

Hydration in the 24-48 Hours Before

Race hydration starts long before race day. In the 24-48 hours beforehand, the goal is simply to reach the start line in an optimal fluid state.

  • Key indicator: pale yellow or almost clear urine through the day. Dark urine means you are dehydrated
  • Recommended amount: 2-3 litres of water across the day before, spread out steadily rather than downed in big volumes
  • Skip the alcohol: alcohol has a diuretic effect that increases fluid loss. Avoid it for the 48 hours before
  • Optional sports drink: if the forecast points to a hot race day, 500ml of sports drink the day before helps you hold on to electrolytes
The urine test: Check the colour of your urine first thing on the day before the race and again last thing at night. Pale yellow means you are in good shape. Dark yellow or amber means you should drink more through the rest of the day.

Race Day: Pre-Start Hydration

Race-day hydration follows its own logic. You want to reach the start line well hydrated, but not with a stomach full of water.

  • 2 hours before the start: 400-500ml of water, sipped slowly alongside breakfast
  • 30 minutes before: another 150-200ml if you are thirsty. Do not force it
  • 20 minutes before: stop drinking. Running the first few kilometres with a full bladder is uncomfortable for no reason
Careful with overhydration: Drinking more than 1 litre of water in the hour before the start can trigger hyponatremia (low sodium) and stomach trouble. Over-drinking is a more common mistake than it looks, especially among runners dealing with pre-race nerves.

Through the 21 Kilometres: Your Aid Station Strategy

Most half marathons have aid stations every 5 km. A solid strategy means drinking at every single one of them, whether you feel thirsty or not. Thirst shows up once dehydration has already started: it is a late signal, not an early one.

Temperature Estimated loss/hour What to drink at each station
Below 10°C 300-500ml/hour Water (100-150ml per station)
10-18°C 500-700ml/hour Alternate water and sports drink
18-25°C 700-900ml/hour Mainly sports drink (150-200ml per station)
Above 25°C 900-1200ml/hour Sports drink at every station (200ml) + extra water if available

The three rules of the aid stations

Rule 1: Drink at every aid station

Do not wait until you are thirsty. Do not skip a water point because you "feel fine". Dehydration builds gradually and performance drops before the brain sends the thirst signal.

Rule 2: Walk for 8-10 seconds and drink properly

Trying to drink at race pace means half of it ends up on your shirt and you swallow more air than you should. Walking 8-10 seconds to drink properly works out at less than 15 seconds of total loss. A small price for hydration that actually lands.

Rule 3: The right amount, neither too little nor too much

The ideal amount at each aid station is between 100 and 200ml. More than 300ml in one go can leave you feeling heavy and uncomfortable while running. Less than 100ml is not enough to cover what you have lost since the last station.

Water vs Sports Drink: Which to Choose

This is one of the most common questions, and the answer depends mainly on how long your race lasts and on the temperature outside.

  • Water: replaces the fluid you lose but not the electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) that leave with your sweat. Enough for races under 1h30 in cool conditions
  • Sports drink: replaces fluid + electrolytes + carbohydrates (roughly 30g per 500ml). Ideal for races over 90 minutes or in the heat

Water only if:

  • You run under 1h30
  • Temperature below 12°C
  • You carry gels that give you carbohydrates and sodium

Alternate water and sports drink if:

  • You run between 1h30 and 2h15
  • Temperature between 12° and 22°C
  • You sweat moderately

Prioritise sports drink if:

  • You run more than 2h15
  • Temperature above 22°C
  • You sweat heavily (salt stains on your kit)

The Mistake of Drinking Too Much (Hyponatremia)

Hyponatremia — low blood sodium caused by taking on too much water without electrolytes — is a real problem for slower runners who finish a half marathon in over 2h30 and drink large amounts of plain water along the way.

Who is most at risk

  • Runners finishing in more than 2h30
  • Runners taking more than 300ml of plain water at every aid station
  • Runners with visibly salty sweat (white stains on their kit)

Symptoms of hyponatremia

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Headache
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Swelling in hands and feet
  • In severe cases: seizures (very rare at these distances)
Prevention: If you are running longer than 2h30, gradually swap water for sports drink from km 10 onwards. Do not take in more than 400-600ml per hour. A sports drink is far safer than plain water on long races.

Post-Race Hydration

Rehydration starts the moment you cross the line. Your body has lost fluid, electrolytes and glycogen, and the first 60 minutes after the race are the most important window for recovery.

  • First hour: 500-750ml of water or sports drink, sipped slowly
  • Rehydration check: if your urine is still dark two hours after finishing, drink more
  • Sodium: eating something salty (salted nuts, a recovery drink, broth) helps your body hold on to the fluid you are drinking
  • Banana: an excellent post-race option thanks to its potassium (a key electrolyte) and recovery carbohydrates
Post-race rule: For every kilogram of body weight lost during the race, drink roughly 1.5 litres of fluid over the following 4-6 hours. Weigh yourself before and after your last long runs to work out your own sweat rate.

Hydration in Different Temperatures

Weather conditions are the factor that changes your hydration strategy the most. The same runner can need twice as much fluid in a 25°C race as in a 10°C one.

Cold — below 10°C

Estimated loss: 300-500ml/hour. Dehydration risk gets underestimated in the cold because you simply feel less thirsty.

  • Drink 100-150ml of water at the km 5, 10, 15 and 20 stations
  • A sports drink is not necessary if the race lasts under 2 hours
  • If you carry gels, water is enough

Cool to mild — 10-22°C

Estimated loss: 500-900ml/hour. The ideal conditions for most half marathons. Go with a balanced strategy.

  • Alternate water and sports drink from km 10
  • 150-200ml at each aid station
  • If you are finishing in over 2 hours, prioritise sports drink over the last 8 km

Heat — above 22°C

Estimated loss: 900-1,200ml/hour. Conditions that call for adjusting your pace and maximising hydration.

  • Sports drink at every aid station from km 5
  • 200ml per station + extra water if it is available to cool down
  • Consider carrying a small bottle if the stations are more than 5km apart
  • Adjust your target pace: heat adds 10 to 30 sec/km to perceived effort

Frequently Asked Questions About Half Marathon Hydration

Should I walk through the aid stations to drink?

Yes, in most cases. Walking for 8-10 seconds so you can actually drink costs you less than 15 seconds of total time, and it stops you choking or spilling the water. Hydration that works pays that time back several times over.

How much water do I lose in a half marathon?

It depends on the heat and the intensity. In cool conditions (10-15°C): 600-900ml per hour. In the heat (above 22°C): up to 1,200ml per hour. A 2-hour half marathon in hot weather can mean a total loss of 2-2.4 litres.

Can I drink only sports drink during the race?

Yes, and it is even better than water alone if the race lasts more than 90 minutes, because it replaces the electrolytes you lose through sweat. A sports drink also lowers the risk of hyponatremia on long races.

Should I carry my own bottle or drink at the aid stations?

It depends on the race. For half marathons with aid stations every 5km, the water points are enough. If it is very hot or you are running slower than 2h30, consider carrying a small bottle or a compact hydration vest.

Is it true that drinking too much water is dangerous?

Yes. Hyponatremia (too much water without electrolytes) can be dangerous in slower runners who drink only water for more than 2-3 hours. Alternate water with a sports drink to keep your sodium balance.

How long before the race should I stop drinking?

Stop drinking 20-30 minutes before the gun. If you drink right before the start, you will run the first few kilometres with a full bladder, which is uncomfortable and may force a toilet stop.

What if nerves stop me drinking the day before?

That is normal. Sip small amounts through the day. The most reliable tool is the colour of your urine: if it is clear or pale yellow, you are well hydrated. Do not force large volumes if you are not thirsty.

Do caffeinated drinks (coffee, cola) count towards hydration?

Coffee in moderate doses (1-2 cups) does not dehydrate you significantly according to current evidence. But do not swap water for coffee the day before the race. Sugary drinks like cola are not a good pre-race option.
