Your First 10K Race: Everything You Need to Know

From picking your first 10K to crossing the finish line. Training plan, nutrition, shoes and race-day strategy.

The 10K was my first race. It was a Sunday in November, it was drizzling, and I finished in 58 minutes feeling invincible. Fourteen years have gone by and I still remember that moment. This guide exists so your first 10K is as memorable as mine — and so you get to the start line with everything you need to know.

Why the 10K Is the Perfect Distance to Start With

The 10K holds a unique place in the recreational running calendar. It is long enough that crossing the finish line feels like a real achievement, and accessible enough that anyone willing to be consistent can prepare for it in a matter of weeks.

The 10 kilometre distance is also the one that improves fastest with training. Watching your times drop in those first months is hugely motivating — going from 60 minutes to 55 in just 8 weeks is perfectly achievable with the right preparation.

8-Week Plan for Your First 10K

This plan is built for someone who can already run between 20 and 30 minutes without stopping. If that is you, 8 weeks will get you ready to finish your first 10K. The plan runs on 3 sessions a week:

Week Session 1 Session 2 Session 3 (long)
Week 1 3 min run / 1 min walk x5 25 min easy jog 4 km continuous
Week 2 5 min run / 1 min walk x4 28 min easy jog 4.5 km continuous
Week 3 30 min continuous 30 min + 2x5min at moderate pace 5 km continuous
Week 4 30 min continuous 35 min + 2x5min at moderate pace 5.5 km continuous
Week 5 35 min continuous 35 min + short intervals 6.5 km continuous
Week 6 40 min continuous 40 min with pace changes 7 km continuous
Week 7 40 min continuous 45 min with pace changes 8 km continuous
Week 8 30 min easy (tapering) 25 min very easy 10K RACE
Key principle: 80% of your training should be easy — at a pace where you can hold a conversation. Only 20% is higher-intensity work. This is the principle beginners ignore most often, and it is the number one cause of injury and dropping out.

How to Choose Your First 10K Race

Not all 10K races are the same. For your first one, these are the criteria that matter most:

  • Pick a local race. Familiar surroundings, family cheering you on and no travel all take the stress out of the day. Your first time should be a positive experience, not a logistics exercise.
  • Avoid technical or hilly courses. A 10K with 200m of elevation gain is far harder than a flat one. For your first, look for a city course or one through a park.
  • The race calendar: There are two big road-race seasons in Spain — spring (March-May) and autumn (September-November). Both are ideal for the temperatures.
  • Sign up at least 6-8 weeks in advance. You need time to prepare, and many popular races have limited places that sell out sooner than you would expect.

To find 10K races near you, check the 2026 road race calendar. If you are in Catalonia or can travel, the Mercè 10K (Barcelona, 20 September) is one of the best road 10Ks to open the autumn: flat city course, more than 12,000 runners and the Festes de la Mercè atmosphere out on the streets.

Nutrition for a 10K

Good news: nutrition for a 10K is far simpler than for longer distances. You do not need gels, carb loading or complicated strategies.

  • The day before: Eat normally and stay well hydrated. There is no need to carb load for 10km.
  • Breakfast (2h before): Something light and familiar — banana with toast, oats with fruit, or milk with cereal. Nothing new, nothing your stomach has not seen before.
  • Water at the aid stations: If the race has aid stations (not every 10K does), take a sip if you need one, but it is not essential.
  • Gels? For runners expecting to finish in more than 50-55 minutes, an optional gel at km 5 can help keep the energy up in the closing stretch. For everyone else, it is not needed.
Golden rule: Never eat or drink anything new on race day. If you have not tested it in training, it does not belong in a race.

What to Expect on Race Day

Race day has its own rhythm. Knowing what is coming takes the nerves away and lets you enjoy it:

  • Arrive 30-45 minutes early. You need time to collect your bib if you do not have it yet, warm up gently and find the right starting pen.
  • Warm-up: 10 minutes of brisk walking and 5 minutes of easy jogging. Do not tire yourself out before the gun.
  • The mass start: The adrenaline of the first kilometre makes everyone go out too fast. It is the most common mistake. Hold back — going 20 seconds per kilometre faster than your target pace in km 1 will cost you double that over the last 3km.
  • Km 1-3: slower than you think you can hold. If you can speak in full sentences, you are on track.
  • Km 5: decision point. If you feel good and your legs are responding, start picking up the pace a little.
  • Km 7-8: the hardest stretch. That is normal. Every runner feels it here, whatever their level. It is physiological and it passes. All you have to do is hold your pace.
  • The last 500 metres: Everything you have left. The finish line is right around the corner.

10K Reference Paces

This table helps you see which performance bracket your target time sits in and what pace per kilometre it takes:

Under 35 min <3:30/km >90% max HR Elite / high performance
Under 40 min <4:00/km 88-92% Advanced
Under 45 min <4:30/km 85-90% Upper intermediate
Under 50 min <5:00/km 80-87% Intermediate
Under 60 min <6:00/km 75-83% Beginner to intermediate
60 min+ >6:00/km 70-78% Beginner

If this is your first race, the goal is not a specific time — it is to finish and enjoy the experience. The time will come on its own, with training and with the races that follow.

Training Groups for Your First 10K

The research on exercise adherence is consistent: people who train in a group drop out far less often than those who train alone. External motivation, social accountability and simply the fact that someone is expecting you get you out of the door on the days you otherwise would not.

For a first 10K, a training group also brings practical knowledge: the right paces, technique, routes, advice on footwear. You will learn more in three group runs than in three weeks of videos.
