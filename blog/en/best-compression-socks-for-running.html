What a compression sock really does

A compression sock applies graduated pressure along the leg: firmer at the ankle, easing off as it climbs towards the knee. That graduated pressure has two documented effects:

  • Venous circulation: it helps venous blood return from the limb towards the heart. Useful during long periods of immobility (flights, standing all day at work) or when the venous system struggles (varicose veins).
  • Muscle support: it reduces muscle vibration on each footstrike. Part of the post-run recovery effect comes from here.

What a compression sock does not do: it does not fix biomechanical problems, it does not replace your warm-up, it does not prevent muscle injuries caused by overtraining and it does not guarantee a single extra watt of performance. Evidence for performance gains during the run is inconsistent across studies. The evidence is more solid for post-effort recovery.

The big marketing misunderstanding: plenty of brands sell compression socks as if they guaranteed better performance during the run. The reality is that studies show mixed results for performance during the activity. The most consistent benefit is for recovery after the run. If you are going to use them mainly to run, the benefit is real but modest. If you are going to use them to recover, the cost-benefit picture is clearer.

How many mmHg you need for each use

The mmHg (millimetres of mercury) measures the pressure the sock applies. This is the practical scale for runners:

  • 8-15 mmHg (very light): the entry level. You barely feel the pressure. Useful for tired legs after long days on your feet. No measurable athletic impact.
  • 15-20 mmHg (light to moderate): the range of technical running socks such as Compressport Pro Racing. Noticeable pressure, calf support while you run. Suitable for sport and for travel.
  • 20-30 mmHg (moderate to firm): the lowest clinical range. More effective for recovery after a long race, for long flights and for legs that tend to swell. CAMBIVO works in this range.
  • 30-40 mmHg and above: medical prescription for venous insufficiency. It makes no sense for recreational running and can be counterproductive without medical supervision.

During the run vs after it: the difference matters

Socks worn during the run

Running compression socks (Compressport Pro Racing, CEP, HANKER) are designed to be worn during the effort. They are thin, breathable and work in the 15-20 mmHg range. The main benefit runners report is less muscle vibration, which can translate into less perceived fatigue in the final kilometres. They also help avoid chafing and blisters on the calf over long distances.

Socks for post-race recovery

The 20-30 mmHg socks such as CAMBIVO or DANISH ENDURANCE Compression are mostly used in the 24-48 hours after a long race or a hard session. Worn at rest, they support venous return and can reduce delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS). They are also useful for flights or long journeys after a race.

The optimal pattern: technical compression socks (15-20 mmHg) during training or racing, plus recovery socks (20-30 mmHg) in the hours afterwards. They are different products with different jobs. Do not use your race-day Compressport to recover on the sofa: they are built for movement, not for long periods at rest.

Frequently asked questions about compression socks for running

How many mmHg do you need in a compression sock for running?

For running during the activity itself, 15-20 mmHg (light to moderate compression) is the usual recommendation. For post-race recovery or air travel, 20-30 mmHg (moderate to firm compression). Above 30 mmHg you are into medical prescription territory, which makes no sense for recreational running. Compressport Pro Racing works in the 15-20 mmHg range; CAMBIVO offers 20-30 mmHg, which suits recovery better than running.

Do compression socks actually improve performance?

The scientific evidence on performance during the run is mixed: some studies show small improvements in running economy and delayed muscle fatigue, others find no significant difference. The evidence is more solid for post-effort recovery: less DOMS and faster recovery over the following 24-48 hours. If you wear them only during the race hoping to beat your personal best, the science is not strongly on your side. If you also use them to recover, the benefit-to-cost ratio makes more sense.

Knee-high sleeve or short compression sock: which is better for running?

It depends on the goal. Knee-high socks apply pressure along the whole calf and are more effective for recovery and long trips. Short compression socks are lighter, cooler and better suited to summer running or to runners who only want support in the foot and ankle. For a marathon or a long trail race in cold weather: knee-high. For 10K sessions in summer: a short or quarter sock.

How long do compression socks last?

Compression degrades with washing and use. Premium brands such as Compressport and CEP tend to keep their properties for 100-150 washes if you wash them at 30°C on a gentle cycle and let them air dry. Cheaper options can lose noticeable compression before 50 washes. The practical rule: if the sock no longer squeezes your calf slightly when you put it on, it has lost its effect.

Can I wear running compression socks on a plane?

Yes, and it is one of their best uses. Prolonged immobility in flight increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis and causes swelling. Socks in the 20-30 mmHg range such as CAMBIVO or DANISH ENDURANCE are ideal for flights longer than 4 hours. For short flights or people with no risk factors, the 15-20 mmHg of Compressport Pro Racing also works. Trail socks such as HANKER are not designed for graduated compression while travelling.

What temperature should you wash compression socks at?

Always 30°C maximum, delicate cycle or by hand. Heat and spinning damage the elastic fibres responsible for the compression. Do not tumble dry. Always air dry, never in direct sun for hours. If you routinely wash at 40°C, you are halving the useful life of the sock.

Do compression socks help with plantar fasciitis?

Compression socks with arch support (such as the DANISH ENDURANCE Running Quarter or the Compressport Run Low) can help reduce tension on the plantar fascia while you run. However, for established plantar fasciitis the first line of treatment is stretching exercises, orthotic insoles if there is pronation, and relative rest. Socks are a complement, not a treatment. If you have had heel pain for more than 3 weeks, see a podiatrist.
